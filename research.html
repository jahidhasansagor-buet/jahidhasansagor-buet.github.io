Research

I predict heat transfer where classical models fail. Three first-author papers, one question: what really happens when heat crosses a nanoscale gap at real operating temperatures?

Applications

This work feeds directly into technologies where thermal design decides performance.

Electronics and data-center cooling

As chips and interconnects shrink toward the nanoscale, classical heat transfer models stop working. Predicting heat flow correctly at these scales is what keeps devices within thermal limits.

Heat-assisted magnetic recording

HAMR drive heads fly sub-nanometer above the disk. Phonon and radiative transport across that gap sets the thermal budget of the whole read-write process.

Near-field energy conversion

Thermophotovoltaic and thermal-management devices exploit radiation that exceeds the blackbody limit at nanoscale gaps. Their design needs material properties at real operating temperatures, not 0 K.

Thermal sensing and scanning probes

Scanning thermal microscopy reads temperature through a tip-sample gap. Interpreting the signal requires knowing how heat actually crosses that gap.

Solar thermal and enclosure design

Solar collectors, solar ponds, and electronic enclosures are natural convection problems with conducting walls. My CFD work maps which geometry, material, and heating choices move the most heat.

Material data for device modeling

Every thermal radiation model needs a dielectric function as input. My framework supplies it at any temperature, for metals, semiconductors, and 2D materials, from first principles.

Ph.D. research

Room-temperature material data breaks down at high temperature. Here is by how much.
Effect of Temperature-Induced Dielectric Response on Radiative Heat Transfer in the Extreme Near-Field Regime · Physical Review B (under review)

Problem

Every prediction of nanoscale thermal radiation depends on one material input: the dielectric function. The whole field computes it at 0 K, then applies it to systems running at 1000 K. Nobody had measured the cost of that shortcut.

What I did

Computed graphene's dielectric function at its actual temperature, 300 to 1200 K, from first principles, with phonon excitations and anharmonicity included. Fed it into NEGF heat transfer calculations.

Result

The 0 K shortcut underpredicts radiative conductance by 32–43% at 1200 K. Anharmonic phonons alone account for up to 25.6% at 750 K. Temperature-true properties are not a refinement. They are the difference between right and wrong.

Tools: NEGF, DFT, anharmonic special displacement method, Quantum ESPRESSO, Yambo, HPC

One workflow. Any material, any temperature, full infrared response.
A Comprehensive Framework for Predicting the Infrared Dielectric Function at Finite Temperatures from First Principles (in preparation)

Problem

The infrared dielectric function drives thermal radiation, sensing, and optoelectronics. Measuring it at every temperature is impractical. Computing it usually stops at 0 K.

What I did

Built a validated first-principles pipeline: Quantum ESPRESSO electronic structure on thermally displaced atoms, Yambo many-body calculations on top. Handles metals, semiconductors, and 2D materials, with harmonic and anharmonic lattice effects.

Result

Tested on graphene, silicon, and gold. Computed silicon absorption at 300 K lands on the experimental data. The static-lattice calculation misses everything below the band gap. The output is temperature-resolved dielectric data that heat transfer models can use directly.

Tools: DFT, DFPT phonons, many-body perturbation theory, SDM/ASDM, Quantum ESPRESSO, EPW, Yambo, HPC

Heat jumps across gaps smaller than an atom. First results for 2D materials.
Phonon Thermal Transport between Two in-Plane, Two-Dimensional Nanoribbons in the Extreme Near-Field Regime · Physical Review B 109, 235411 (2024) · DOI

Problem

At sub-nanometer separations, lattice vibrations carry heat straight across a vacuum gap. This sets thermal limits in heat-assisted magnetic recording and scanning probe microscopy, and it controls how cracks and voids block heat in materials. For 2D materials, no one had the numbers.

What I did

Modeled phonon conductance between graphene and silicene nanoribbons with the atomistic Green's function method, verified against three independent published benchmarks first.

Result

A 0.2 Å gap, one fifth of a bond length, already cuts conductance by over an order of magnitude. The decay is exponential in three regimes set by the interatomic forces: e−10.0d, e−2.0d, then e−1.3d. Silicene overtakes graphene beyond 0.8 Å because of its longer bonds.

Tools: Atomistic Green's function method, Tersoff and Lennard-Jones potentials, MATLAB, HPC

Prior research: CFD

Spin the cylinders the right way and heat transfer goes up. Here is the recipe.
Effect of Parameters of Rotating Cylinders on the Heat Transfer Advancement · Journal of Heat Transfer 144, 022601 (2022) · DOI

Problem

Enclosures with rotating parts show up in MEMS devices, nuclear systems, and food processing. Rotation direction, cylinder position, material, and speed all affect the heat transfer, but nobody had mapped which combination wins.

What I did

Finite element simulations of conjugate mixed convection in a differentially heated square cavity with two rotating conductive cylinders. Swept Re from 1 to 10,000 across four rotation combinations, three positions, five materials, and five speed ratios. Validated against two published studies first.

Result

Both cylinders counterclockwise wins: 2.63% more heat transfer than both clockwise. Position adds another 2.17% (cylinders at 0.75H). Rotating beats stationary by 4%. Cylinder material barely matters. The paper delivers Nu–Re correlations engineers can plug into design directly.

Tools: Galerkin finite element method, conjugate mixed convection, code validation against literature

Which wall shape and material actually move more heat? We tested 16 combinations.
Evaluation of Overall Thermal Performance for Conjugate Natural Convection in a Trapezoidal Cavity with Different Surface Corrugations · International Communications in Heat and Mass Transfer 130, 105772 (2022) · DOI · 44 citations

Problem

Trapezoidal enclosures with thick, textured walls appear in solar ponds and food processing. Corrugation shape and wall material both change the heat transfer, but their combined effect had never been tested.

What I did

Finite element simulations of conjugate natural convection across four corrugation shapes (flat, rectangular, sinusoidal, triangular) and four wall materials (glass fiber, pinewood, plexiglas, dry concrete), Ra from 10³ to 10⁶. Validated against both numerical and experimental published data.

Result

The answer depends on what you optimize. For convection alone, flat surface with glass fiber wins. For overall conjugate performance, the ranking flips: triangular corrugation with dry concrete is best. Below Ra = 10⁴ conduction rules everything. A useful warning for designers: picking by one metric can give you the wrong wall.

Tools: Galerkin finite element method, COMSOL Multiphysics, conjugate natural convection

Same cavity, same fluid, three ways to heat it. Only one wins at high Rayleigh number.
Entropy Analysis due to Conjugate Natural Convection in Water-Al₂O₃ Nanofluid Filled Prismatic Cavity · AIP Conference Proceedings 2324, 050022 (2021) · DOI

Problem

Prismatic enclosures appear in solar collectors, greenhouses, and electronics cooling. Filling them with nanofluid boosts heat transfer, but nobody had studied a nanofluid-filled prismatic cavity with a thick conducting wall, or asked which heating profile on that wall performs best.

What I did

Solved conjugate natural convection of water-Al₂O₃ nanofluid in a prismatic cavity with a thick pinewood bottom wall using the Galerkin finite element method. Compared three heating profiles, isothermal, linear, and sinusoidal, across Ra from 10³ to 10⁷, tracking heat transfer and entropy generation together. Validated against published square-cavity results first.

Result

Sinusoidal heating delivers the highest heat transfer once convection takes over (Ra ≥ 2×10⁵), while linear heating always loses. Entropy generation stays flat up to Ra = 3×10⁴ and peaks for isothermal heating. So the best-mixing condition and the most-wasteful condition are not the same one, and that matters for design.

Tools: Galerkin finite element method, nanofluid modeling, conjugate natural convection, entropy generation analysis
